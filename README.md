### **Job Market Indicators**

### **Unemployment Rate**

**What it is:** The percentage of the labor force that is jobless and actively seeking employment.

**What it measures:** Labor market health and the economy's ability to provide jobs. Calculated as (unemployed workers / total labor force) × 100.

**Why it matters:** High unemployment signals economic weakness; low unemployment indicates a strong economy. It influences consumer spending power and Federal Reserve policy decisions.

### **Initial Jobless Claims**

**What it is:** The number of people filing for unemployment benefits for the first time in a given week.

**What it measures:** Real-time health of the job market and emerging trends in employment.

**Why it matters:** Rising claims can signal economic deterioration before it shows up in monthly unemployment data. It's a leading indicator that provides early warning signals.

### **Nonfarm Payrolls**

**What it is:** The total number of paid workers in the U.S., excluding farm employees, government employees, private household employees, and nonprofit organization employees.

**What it measures:** Monthly change in employment and job creation across the economy.

**Why it matters:** One of the most watched economic indicators. Strong job growth indicates economic expansion, while declining payrolls signal contraction. Heavily influences Fed policy and market movements.

### **Inflation Indicators**

### **CPI (Consumer Price Index) - Year over Year**

**What it is:** A measure of the average change in prices paid by consumers for a basket of goods and services over time.

**What it measures:** The rate of inflation from the consumer's perspective, comparing prices to the same month in the previous year.

**Why it matters:** High CPI growth erodes purchasing power and often leads to Fed rate hikes. Investors watch this to anticipate monetary policy changes and protect against inflation.

### **Core CPI (Year over Year)**

**What it is:** CPI excluding volatile food and energy prices.

**What it measures:** Underlying inflation trends without short-term price fluctuations in food and energy.

**Why it matters:** Considered a better indicator of long-term inflation trends. The Fed focuses heavily on Core CPI when making interest rate decisions.

### **PPI (Producer Price Index) - Month over Month**

**What it is:** Measures the average change in selling prices received by domestic producers for their output.

**What it measures:** Inflation at the wholesale/producer level before it reaches consumers.

**Why it matters:** Leading indicator of consumer inflation. Rising PPI often precedes rising CPI as producers pass costs to consumers. Helps predict future CPI movements.

### **Economic Activity Indicators**

### **ISM Manufacturing PMI**

**What it is:** Purchasing Managers' Index based on surveys of manufacturing sector executives about business conditions.

**What it measures:** Manufacturing sector health including new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, and inventories.

**Why it matters:** Reading above 50 indicates expansion; below 50 signals contraction. Leading indicator of economic health since manufacturing often shifts before the broader economy.

### **ISM Non-Manufacturing PMI**

**What it is:** PMI focused on the services sector (which represents ~80% of the U.S. economy).

**What it measures:** Business activity, new orders, employment, and prices in services industries.

**Why it matters:** Services dominate the modern economy, so this indicator often has more impact than manufacturing PMI. Signals strength or weakness in the largest portion of GDP.

### **Chicago PMI**

**What it is:** Regional PMI measuring business conditions in the Chicago area.

**What it measures:** Production, new orders, order backlogs, employment, and supplier deliveries in the Chicago region.

**Why it matters:** Released before national ISM data, it provides an early read on manufacturing conditions. Chicago's diverse industrial base makes it a useful proxy for national trends.

### **Consumer Confidence**

**What it is:** Index measuring how optimistic consumers feel about the economy and their personal financial situation.

**What it measures:** Consumer sentiment about current conditions and expectations for the next six months.

**Why it matters:** Consumer spending drives ~70% of U.S. GDP. High confidence typically leads to increased spending, while low confidence signals potential economic slowdown. Leading indicator of future consumer behavior.
